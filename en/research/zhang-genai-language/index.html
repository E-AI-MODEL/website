EAI · Research review of Zhang et al. (2026)

Does AI help students learn another language?

A meta-analysis of 48 studies reports a positive average effect. We added up the participant counts, checked selected original studies and examined some unusual statistics. One finding stands out: an unexplained difference of 818 participants between the table and the main text. What do these results really tell us about learning with AI?

Hans Visser · 14 November 2026 · English · 9 pages

Read the review (PDF, 9 pages)Lees in het Nederlands

The average effect is positive. But how large?

Zhang and colleagues combine 48 studies and 67 effect sizes. In many of those studies, students using AI scored higher on the measured language outcomes. The pooled effect is g = 0.76. Remove three exceptionally large results and it falls to g = 0.57. Still positive, but noticeably smaller.

The results also vary considerably (I² = 92.64%). Practising a conversation with a chatbot is different from revising a text with AI feedback. One average cannot tell a teacher what will happen in a particular classroom.

We checked the participant counts

Table 2 lists the sample sizes for all 48 studies, across pages 9 to 12. They add up to 4,378 participants. The main text reports 5,196. What accounts for the difference of 818?

A difference of 818. The paper does not explain it. Different counting rules or a reporting error are possible. The group-level data are needed to find out.

We went back to the original studies

In Bašić, students wrote in Croatian, their first language, even though the meta-analysis focuses on second-language learning. Alenezi is classified as a randomized experiment by Zhang, while the original authors describe it as quasi-experimental. Two of the three unusually large effects come from an Amazon Alexa study. Whether that particular application meets the paper's generative-AI criteria still needs checking.

We also checked some of the statistics. The t-value reported by Y. Wang (2025) cannot be reproduced as the same comparison from the published group figures. In Zhang's paper, one p-value does not match the stated degrees of freedom. The review works through possible explanations without claiming more than the evidence supports.

What can students still do without AI?

Only four of the 48 studies include a delayed post-test. Most measured performance during or soon after instruction. Those results matter, but they tell us much less about what students can do weeks later without AI, or on a new task.

The article reports an average advantage on the outcomes measured. Whether this translates into lasting language proficiency needs more evidence. The population also matters: 39 of the 48 studies took place in higher education.

Read the full source review

The nine-page report includes our count of Table 2, checks against the original studies, recalculations and references. It also explains what remains unresolved. This is not a full reproduction of all 67 effect sizes. That requires the underlying data and analysis code.
