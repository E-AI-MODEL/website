Essay · AI and provenance

what the watermark?!

How textGrain may affect the quality of a ChatGPT answer.

22 November 2026 · Hans Visser

Ask ChatGPT to explain something. You expect an answer that is accurate, clear and carefully worded. On 5 October 2026, OpenAI announced that it would introduce textGrain in phases for eligible ChatGPT and Codex text output in the EU. That does not mean every ChatGPT answer carries a watermark. Nothing is inserted into the text. Instead, textGrain subtly changes the probabilities of certain words as the answer is written. [1]

It sounds harmless enough. But the watermark intervenes in the very process that shapes the answer. How can we tell whether making an answer identifiable comes at a cost to its quality?

I have been interested in this question for some time. For more than a year and a half, I have experimented with hidden text, white letters on a white background, acrostics and recognisable patterns in prose and code. Later, I began working with digital signatures and verifiable provenance. My early experiments taught me something simple: finding a trace is not the same as knowing what it proves.

A watermark in the choice of words

A language model does not produce an entire answer in one go. It generates text one token at a time. A token may be a word or part of one. At each step, the model calculates the probabilities of possible next tokens. textGrain, the method OpenAI introduced on 5 October 2026, intervenes at precisely this point.

OpenAI constructs several slightly modified probability distributions. A secret key determines which distribution is used for a particular choice. The distributions are designed so that their average matches the original probabilities. Over a longer passage, those choices can leave a statistically detectable pattern. [2]

A different token does not necessarily produce a worse answer. Often, several wordings are equally good. Yet the chosen words also become the context for the next prediction. That alone makes the effect of watermarking on answers worth testing.

OpenAI describes an adjustable trade-off between watermark strength and the variation available in generated answers. There is a practical question behind that setting. The signal needs to be detectable later, but it should not unnecessarily constrain the model while it writes. [2]

What does OpenAI's research say about quality?

OpenAI compared the performance of its Astra model with and without textGrain. According to the company, there was no meaningful overall performance difference across the benchmarks it tested. Some individual scores were slightly lower with the watermark; others were slightly higher. These are OpenAI's own measurements, not independent proof that all ChatGPT answers remain equally good. OpenAI considers these differences consistent with ordinary measurement variability. These figures do not establish that the watermark either improves or harms performance. [1]

OpenAI also reports no change in the share of negative user ratings in earlier ChatGPT tests. That is reassuring, and it deserves to be acknowledged. It does not tell us how every individual answer changes, or what happens in tasks where a precise qualification or a particular choice of words matters. [2]

Those are the questions I would like to examine. Does an explanation remain equally complete? Is a necessary qualification preserved? Does the same prompt produce more or less variation across repeated answers? Two different outputs are not evidence of a watermark effect. We need to measure the model's normal variation and compare it with its variation when textGrain is enabled.

How much detectability do we gain?

The other part of the question concerns detection. The watermark is introduced so that the involvement of an OpenAI system may be recognised later. But the detector does not always recover the signal.

At a target false-positive rate of 1%, OpenAI detected the watermark in roughly 80% of certain 200-token passages. For 400-token passages, the figure rose to about 95%. Detection was considerably lower in mathematical answers, where there is less freedom to choose different words. [1]

Editing matters too. In a test using 400-token English passages, detection fell from roughly 92% to 66% when 10% of the words were replaced with synonyms. With 25% replaced, it fell to approximately 17%. [1]

A watermark therefore faces two separate tests. Can we still detect it after a text has been used and edited? And does embedding it affect the quality of the answer? Improving the detection score alone cannot answer the second question.

What I had already found with IdeaLens

In October, I wrote an essay titled IdeaLens, een interessante kip-ei-detectietool, roughly, IdeaLens: an intriguing chicken-and-egg detector. IdeaLens does not primarily try to identify who wrote the words. It attempts to identify where the ideas came from. That is an interesting shift. My concern was whether the detector could actually measure what the phrase came from implies.

The system first uses a language model to construct an outline of a text's content. Arguments, events and conclusions receive brief descriptions. Much of the original wording disappears. IdeaLens then predicts a provenance label from that outline. It is an ingenious way to reduce reliance on writing style. [3]

But how did the system learn what counts as a human idea? The researchers used approximately one million documents initially labelled by Pangram 3.3.2. Pangram estimates the provenance of the prose. IdeaLens learns from those labels to make predictions about the ideas. The authors call them silver labels: potentially useful assumptions, not direct observations of the moment an idea was conceived. [3]

That is the chicken-and-egg problem in my earlier essay. An incorrect label about the words can become part of the training signal for a claim about the ideas. Even a correct label showing that AI wrote the prose would not establish that AI devised the arguments. The researchers recognise this problem and also conduct controlled experiments in which the source of an outline is known in advance. That is a substantial improvement over relying on inherited labels alone.

In one such experiment, AI wrote full texts from detailed outlines supplied by humans. Pangram 4 still flagged 92.4% of those texts as AI, while IdeaLens flagged just 6.8%. The reverse experiment is equally revealing. Fifty people wrote stories following an outline generated by AI. IdeaLens flagged 34 of the fifty stories as containing AI-originated ideas; Pangram 4 flagged only four as AI text. The human participants were instructed to stay close to the supplied outline. These experiments show that IdeaLens responds to the source of substantive guidance. They do not establish that every form of human–AI collaboration can be fairly reduced to two labels. [3]

My criticism went beyond those headline numbers. IdeaLens uses a language model to produce the content outline, and a second pass need not yield precisely the same outline. The researchers repeated the extraction five times per document. For randomly selected documents, the label stayed the same in all five runs in 94% of cases. Among documents close to the decision boundary, that fell to 52.5%. There, the same unchanged text can receive a different classification simply because the analysis was repeated. [3]

I used the Dutch term binairproductie for reducing such a writing process to the two labels human or AI. It is my term, not a technical concept from the paper. A person might frame the question, ask AI for suggestions, reject those suggestions and then develop their own argument. The finished text does not disclose who made which substantive decision at each point. A binary label cannot reconstruct that history.

What if a watermark passes through an idea detector?

That returns us to textGrain. OpenAI embeds a statistical pattern in token selection as the model writes. IdeaLens, in turn, asks a language model to transform those words into a content outline. What, if anything, of the original pattern survives that transformation?

Possibly nothing. It is also conceivable that a different wording changes which nuance the outline preserves, giving what appears to be the same underlying idea a different label. That is a hypothesis, not an observed effect. I raised this question in my earlier IdeaLens analysis. The published studies do not establish that textGrain changes IdeaLens predictions.

We could test it using answers generated from the same prompts, both with and without textGrain. Run the resulting texts through IdeaLens repeatedly, generating and classifying new outlines each time. Compare substantive differences, the stability of the labels and the detectability of the watermark. That would help distinguish a genuine change in content from a different summary or a random shift near the detector's threshold.

OpenAI, show us the other half of the experiment

I also study how the provenance of digital information can be checked. Through our Emmauscollege workspace, we have been granted access to OpenAI Daybreak Blue for authorised defensive security research. Its activation is documented internally. That explains some of my interest in the subject; it is not an independent evaluation of textGrain and gives us no access to OpenAI's internal watermarking code. [4]

OpenAI has shown that its model's average performance did not meaningfully change in the published benchmarks. That is useful evidence. I am asking for a more precise experiment. Have the model answer the same questions repeatedly with and without textGrain, at different watermark strengths. Independently assess factual accuracy, completeness and necessary qualifications. Include Dutch-language prompts and tasks where there is little room for alternative wording. Publish the spread of results, not only their averages.

Then examine the second question from my IdeaLens essay. Feed those paired answers through IdeaLens, repeat the outline extractions and track where the labels shift. Can the shift be traced to a change in content, a different summary or ordinary variation within the detector? The experiment might support the hypothesis or rule it out. Either outcome would tell us something.

My request to OpenAI and other researchers is specific: make the relationship between watermark detectability and answer quality open to scrutiny, and publish the cases in which detection is unstable. The available figures do not justify saying that textGrain reduces answer quality. They also leave questions about individual answers and the interpretation of subsequent detection unresolved.

And to anyone considering a detector for student work, a publication or an authorship dispute: before treating a score as evidence, ask what the system actually measured, how repeatable the result is and what is known about the writing process. A statistical label is not a record of someone's thinking.

Do not just show that a watermark can be detected. Show what embedding it changes in the answer.

My first watermark was hidden in white text. Now we are discussing the choice of the next token. Technically, that is a major change. The question remains familiar: what does the signal really prove, and what did we alter to put it there?

Hans Visser
