Start with the learning.

You can judge what AI should do only when you know what a student needs to learn at this point. EAI organises that decision into four steps.

01Process02Stage03Meaningful action04AI

Each step below uses the same example: a student learning to compare two historical sources.

01
Process

What should the student learn?

What should the student ultimately know or be able to do, and how do they get there?

Look beyond the submitted work. Working with sources may involve reading, recognising perspectives, weighing arguments and supporting a judgement. Identify where instruction, practice and independent work are needed.

The source example

Supporting a historical judgement

The student needs to learn to recognise and weigh differences between sources. The written comparison is one result of that learning process, not the entire goal.

02
Stage

Where is the student now?

Where is the student in that learning process?

A student may be following an example, practising with help, responding to feedback or working independently. The same AI feature may have a different role in each situation.

The source example

From instruction to making a comparison

A worked comparison may help during instruction. During independent practice, the student needs to learn to find the relevant differences themselves.

Instruction→Practice→Independent use
03
Meaningful action · Kernhandeling

What must the student make sense of?

Which step must the student make sense of themselves at this stage for learning to take place?

This is not automatically the hardest or longest step. Adding something visible is not proof either: a student can change sentences without understanding what needs to change in the reasoning. Following an explanation can be meaningful without producing new work.

The source example

Deciding which difference matters

During independent comparison, the student weighs which differences relate to the authors' positions and what those differences mean for their judgement.

During instruction, the meaningful action may instead be following and making sense of a demonstrated argument.

04
AI

What does AI do here?

What does AI do at this particular point?

Examine what AI offers, demonstrates or takes over. A hint may already supply the very step the student needs to find. A long explanation need not take that step away. What the help contains matters, not just how much help is given.

The source example

Reconsidering the same worked answer

A complete AI comparison may fit instruction. During independent practice, that same answer may take over the comparison. Specify which help fits now and what the student should do themselves afterwards.

After AI assistance

What do you know about the learning?

A good result with help does not yet tell you what the student can do independently. Keep these four questions separate.

  • With help

    Does the task succeed with assistance?

  • Independently

    Can the student perform the action without the same help afterwards?

  • Later

    Can they still do it at a later time?

  • In a different situation

    Can they apply it to different sources or a new question?

Two kinds of knowledge

Understanding learning and understanding AI

You need to know what the student should learn at this stage. You also need to understand which questions, choices and judgements AI can supply. Both kinds of knowledge inform the choice of assistance.

What about professional work?

Learning is not the goal of every task. Quality, time and responsibility for a decision may come first. Do not preserve an action merely because it used to be done by hand; judge the division of work against the goal.

The reasoning behind the model

Read further and check the sources

EAI organises a professional judgement. It is not an effect measure and does not automatically approve an AI application. The publications and sources allow you to examine the reasoning.
